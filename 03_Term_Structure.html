Term Structure of Interest Rates

Yield curve shapes, term structure theories, Nelson-Siegel model fitting, and PCA curve dynamics

The Geometry of the Yield Curve

The term structure of interest rates plots sovereign bond yields against maturity, spanning from overnight cash out to 30 or 50 years. The shape of this curve reflects the macroeconomic consensus on future growth, inflation expectations, central bank policy cycles, and the compensation demanded by investors for bearing duration risk (the Term Premium).

The Four Canonical Curve Regimes

Curve Shape Slope Metric ($2s10s$) Macroeconomic Interpretation Typical Market Regime
Normal (Upward Sloping) $10\text{Y} - 2\text{Y} > +50\text{ bps}$ Healthy economic expansion, positive term premium, stable inflation expectation. Mid-cycle growth or early recovery.
Steep Curve $10\text{Y} - 2\text{Y} > +150\text{ bps}$ Central bank cutting rates aggressively to stimulate economy; long rates anticipate future recovery. Post-recession bull steepener or reflationary fiscal surge.
Flat Curve $-15\text{ bps} \le 10\text{Y} - 2\text{Y} \le +15\text{ bps}$ Monetary policy tightening taking hold; economic momentum decelerating. Late-cycle transition phase.
Inverted (Downward Sloping) $10\text{Y} - 2\text{Y} < -15\text{ bps}$ Restrictive policy braking the economy; markets pricing aggressive future rate cuts to combat recession. Late-stage central bank overtightening; classic recession signal.
Humped Curve Medium-term ($2\text{Y}-5\text{Y}$) peak Terminal policy rate peak expected in near term followed by easing in distant years. Peak tightening cycle (e.g. 2006, 2023).

The Inversion Transmission Mechanism

Historically, every US recession since 1955 has been preceded by an inversion of the $10\text{Y}-2\text{Y}$ or $10\text{Y}-3\text{M}$ Treasury curve with a median lag of 12 to 18 months.

⚠️
Why Inversion Breaks the Real Economy: Commercial banks borrow short-term (paying rates tied to 3M cash/SOFR) and lend long-term (mortgages, term loans tied to 5Y-10Y). When the curve inverts, bank Net Interest Margin (NIM) collapses, prompting banks to tighten credit lending standards, restricting corporate capital expenditure and triggering economic slowdown.

Theories of the Term Structure

Why does the yield curve slope upward most of the time? Over the last century, economists and quantitative desks developed four complementary theories to explain the shape of the yield curve.

1. Pure Expectations Hypothesis (PEH)

Forward rates are unbiased predictors of future spot rates. Investors are risk-neutral, viewing bonds of all maturities as perfect substitutes:

Pure Expectations Hypothesis Formulation
  • Upward sloping curve: Implies market expects interest rates to rise in the future.
  • Downward sloping curve: Implies market expects interest rates to fall.
  • Flaw: Empirically fails because investors are risk-averse and require risk compensation (the Term Premium). Long bonds consistently offer higher average returns than rolled short bills over multi-decade horizons.

2. Liquidity Preference Theory (LPT)

Investors prefer holding liquid short-term debt over locking up capital in long-term debt, which is exposed to price volatility. Borrowers (governments, corporations), however, prefer borrowing long-term.

To induce investors to hold long-term bonds, issuers must pay a positive, monotonically increasing Liquidity / Term Premium ($TP_t > 0$):

Liquidity Preference Formulation
💡
Key S&T Insight: Under Liquidity Preference, an upward-sloping yield curve does not necessarily mean interest rates will rise! If the term premium is +150 bps and expected future rates are flat or even falling slightly (-50 bps), the observable nominal curve will still slope upward (+100 bps)!

3. Market Segmentation Theory (MST)

Investors and issuers are confined to specific maturity buckets by regulatory mandates or liability matching constraints:

  • Commercial banks and corporate cash treasuries operate strictly in the short end ($0-2\text{Y}$).
  • Pension funds and life insurers with 30-year liability cash flows operate strictly in the ultra-long end ($20-30\text{Y}$).
  • Yields in each maturity bucket are determined purely by local supply and demand, with zero substitution across tenors.

4. Preferred Habitat Theory (PHT)

A realistic compromise between Pure Expectations and Segmentation. Investors have a "preferred habitat" (their natural liability tenor), but will cross into adjacent maturity sectors if offered an attractive risk premium. The yield curve reflects both expectations of future rates and supply/demand imbalances across maturity sectors.

Parametric Curve Construction: The Nelson-Siegel Model

In quantitative finance and central banking (Federal Reserve, ECB, Bank of England), fitting smooth, continuous zero-coupon yield curves from discrete market bond quotes requires robust parametric modeling. The gold standard is the Nelson-Siegel (1987) model.

Mathematical Formulation

The zero-coupon yield $y(\tau)$ for maturity $\tau$ is expressed as a linear combination of three intuitive shape components plus an exponential decay factor $\lambda$:

Nelson-Siegel Functional Form

Economic Interpretation of the Parameters

Parameter Economic Role Weight / Loading Function Asymptotic Limits
$\beta_0$ Level Factor (Long-term rate) $L_0(\tau) = 1$ (constant across all maturities) $\lim_{\tau \to \infty} y(\tau) = \beta_0$
$\beta_1$ Slope Factor (Short-term spread) $L_1(\tau) = \frac{1 - e^{-\tau/\lambda}}{\tau/\lambda}$ (decays monotonically from 1 to 0) $\lim_{\tau \to 0} y(\tau) = \beta_0 + \beta_1$
$\beta_2$ Curvature Factor (Medium-term hump) $L_2(\tau) = \frac{1 - e^{-\tau/\lambda}}{\tau/\lambda} - e^{-\tau/\lambda}$ (starts at 0, peaks at $\tau \approx \lambda$, decays to 0) Peaks at intermediate maturities ($2-5\text{Y}$)
$\lambda$ Decay Parameter Determines the maturity $\tau$ where the curvature loading reaches its maximum Typically $\lambda \approx 1.5 - 3.0$ in sovereign debt

The Svensson (1994) Extension

Curve Dynamics & Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

In rates sales & trading, bond prices do not move independently across maturities. Historical empirical analysis of sovereign curves reveals that over 99% of all daily yield curve movements can be explained by just three orthogonal factors:

Component Name Variance Explained Trading Desk Meaning
PC 1 Level (Parallel Shift) ~85% – 90% All yields shift up or down in unison. Driven by aggregate macroeconomic inflation shocks or broad monetary policy stance changes.
PC 2 Slope (Steepener / Flattener) ~8% – 10% Curve pivots around the 5Y–7Y sector. Short rates move by more than long rates. Driven by central bank policy surprises.
PC 3 Curvature (Butterfly) ~2% – 4% The "belly" of the curve ($5\text{Y}$) moves relative to the "wings" ($2\text{Y}$ and $10\text{Y}/30\text{Y}$). Traded via Butterfly spreads.

Sales & Trading, Structuring & CFA L1/L2 Interview Flashcards

These questions are frequently asked by fixed income desks during rates trader, strategist, and quant structuring interviews, as well as on CFA exams.

1. What does an inverted yield curve signal, and what is the economic transmission mechanism to recession? +

Answer: An inverted yield curve (where short rates exceed long rates, such as $10\text{Y}-2\text{Y} < 0$) is the most reliable leading indicator of recession in modern financial history.

The Transmission Mechanism:

  1. Restrictive Policy Setting: The central bank has hiked policy rates above the neutral rate ($r^*$) to suppress inflation, pushing short-term funding costs very high.
  2. Expectation of Future Easing: Markets anticipate that high rates will slow economic growth and reduce inflation, forcing the central bank to cut rates substantially in future years (dragging down long yields).
  3. Banking NIM Compression & Credit Contraction: Commercial banks fund loans by borrowing short (deposits, money markets) and lending long (mortgages, business term credit). An inverted curve destroys bank Net Interest Margins (NIM). Banks respond by sharply tightening underwriting standards and restricting loan availability, starving businesses of working capital and precipitating an economic downturn.
2. Explain the difference between a Bull Steepener and a Bear Steepener. When does a trading desk position for each? +

Answer: Both trades result in an increase in the $10\text{Y}-2\text{Y}$ spread, but they occur under completely opposite market environments:

  • Bull Steepener (Rates Fall): Short rates collapse faster than long rates. This occurs when economic growth stalls and the central bank begins emergency rate cuts. Prices rally across the board, led by front-end bonds. A desk positions for this when anticipating the start of an easing cycle (buying 2Y Treasuries/SOFR futures).
  • Bear Steepener (Rates Rise): Long rates surge much faster than short rates. This occurs during reflation, rising long-term inflation expectations, or heavy fiscal deficit debt issuance that overwhelms investor demand for duration. Front-end rates remain pinned by the central bank while 10Y/30Y yields spike. A desk positions for this by shorting long-end duration or paying fixed on 10Y/30Y swaps.
3. Walk me through the 3 principal components of yield curve movements (PCA). +

Answer: Principal Component Analysis (PCA) decomposes the covariance matrix of daily yield changes across maturities into three orthogonal eigenvectors:

  1. PC1: Level (Parallel Shift): Explains 85% to 90% of total curve variance. All yields move in the same direction with roughly equal magnitude. Reflects global macroeconomic conditions and overall inflation shocks.
  2. PC2: Slope (Twist / Steepener / Flattener): Explains 8% to 10% of total variance. The curve pivots: short rates rise while long rates fall (or vice-versa). Driven by monetary policy cycle expectations.
  3. PC3: Curvature (Butterfly Shift): Explains 2% to 4% of variance. The "belly" (5Y) moves in the opposite direction of the "wings" (2Y and 10Y/30Y). Driven by supply imbalances, hedging flows, or intermediate liquidity preferences.
Trader Tip: Rates desks hedge risk using PCA factor exposures (Level DV01, Slope DV01, Curvature DV01) rather than looking only at flat portfolio duration.
4. Explain the Nelson-Siegel model and the financial interpretation of its three beta parameters. +

Answer: The Nelson-Siegel model fits a continuous zero-coupon yield curve using a parsimonious four-parameter equation:

$y(\tau) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \left( \frac{1 - e^{-\tau/\lambda}}{\tau/\lambda} \right) + \beta_2 \left( \frac{1 - e^{-\tau/\lambda}}{\tau/\lambda} - e^{-\tau/\lambda} \right)$

  • $\beta_0$ (Level): Represents the long-term asymptotic rate ($\lim_{\tau \to \infty} y(\tau) = \beta_0$). Its loading is constant at 1.
  • $\beta_1$ (Slope): Represents the short-to-long spread. As $\tau \to 0$, $y(0) = \beta_0 + \beta_1$, meaning $\beta_1 = y(0) - y(\infty)$. A negative $\beta_1$ indicates an upward-sloping yield curve.
  • $\beta_2$ (Curvature): Represents the medium-term hump. Its loading starts at 0, reaches a maximum at $\tau \approx 1.793\lambda$, and decays to 0. A positive $\beta_2$ creates an upward hump in intermediate yields.
  • $\lambda$ (Decay Parameter): Governs the maturity at which the curvature loading reaches its peak.
5. What is the Pure Expectations Hypothesis, and why does empirical evidence consistently reject it? +

Answer: The Pure Expectations Hypothesis (PEH) posits that forward rates are unbiased predictors of future spot rates, meaning:

$f_{t, T} = \mathbb{E}_t[s_T]$

It assumes investors are strictly risk-neutral and duration risk carries zero risk premium.

Why Empirical Evidence Rejects It:

  • Fama-Bliss (1987) & Campbell-Shiller (1991): Regressions of yield changes on forward-spot spreads repeatedly show that forward rates systematically overestimate future spot rates.
  • Persistent Positive Term Premium: Because investors are risk-averse, they demand a positive Term Premium ($TP > 0$) to bear the volatility and inflation risk of long-term bonds. Rolling short bills over 30 years yields lower average returns than holding 30-year Treasuries.
6. What is a Butterfly trade (e.g., 2s5s10s), and how do rates traders construct a duration-neutral butterfly? +

Answer: A butterfly trade is a relative value rates strategy involving three maturity points: two "wings" (e.g. 2Y and 10Y) and a "body" or "belly" (e.g. 5Y).

  • Long Butterfly: Buy the wings (2Y and 10Y) and sell the body (5Y). The trader profits if the curve becomes more humped (belly yield rises relative to wings).
  • Short Butterfly: Sell the wings and buy the body. The trader profits if the curve flattens out or becomes less humped.
  • Duration & Money Neutrality: To isolate pure curvature risk without being exposed to parallel curve shifts (level) or slope pivots, the position is weighted so that: $$\text{DV01}_{\text{belly}} = \text{DV01}_{\text{wing 1}} + \text{DV01}_{\text{wing 2}}$$ Desks frequently use a "50/50" or regression-weighted weighting to ensure both Level and Slope beta are neutralized.
7. How do rates trading desks build a multi-curve framework using deposits, futures, and swaps? +

Answer: To construct an accurate, smooth discount and projection curve without maturity gaps, desks combine the most liquid benchmark instruments across maturity sectors (the "instrument strip"):

  • Front End ($0 - 3\text{M}$): Overnight Index Swaps (OIS) fixings (SOFR / ESTR) and cash interbank deposits. Used to anchor the overnight discount factor.
  • Short-to-Medium End ($3\text{M} - 2\text{Y}$): STIR futures contracts (3-Month SOFR futures or Euribor futures). Convexity adjustments are applied to convert future rates to forward rates.
  • Long End ($2\text{Y} - 30\text{Y}/50\text{Y}$): Par plain-vanilla interest rate swaps (IRS). Standard annual or semi-annual fixed vs. floating swaps.
Post-2008 Standard: Modern desks use a Dual-Curve Framework: cash flows are discounted using the risk-free OIS curve (SOFR/ESTR), while floating coupon projections are generated from the tenor reference curve.
8. What is the Term Premium, and how do models like Adrian-Crump-Moench (ACM) estimate it? +

Answer: The Term Premium ($TP$) is the excess yield an investor requires to hold a long-term Treasury bond instead of rolling a sequence of short-term bills over the same horizon:

y_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \mathbb{E}[r_{t+i}] + TP_n

  • Drivers: Inflation uncertainty, supply/demand imbalances (e.g. Fed Quantitative Easing suppresses term premium; heavy US Treasury issuance elevates it).
  • The ACM Model (NY Fed): An affine term structure model that extracts the unobservable expected policy rate path from historical pricing and macroeconomic variables, isolating the residual as the estimated Term Premium. A negative term premium indicates that long Treasuries are so prized as hedging assets that investors accept yields lower than the expected future cash rate!
9. What is a Bear Flattener, and why is it considered the most dangerous environment for equity markets? +

Answer: In a Bear Flattener, yields across the curve rise, but front-end yields (2Y) surge significantly faster than long-end yields (10Y), causing the curve to flatten rapidly.

  • Cause: Aggressive, surprise central bank monetary tightening to battle sticky inflation (e.g., Federal Reserve in 2022).
  • Impact on Equities: It creates a "double squeeze": (1) The rising discount rate (risk-free rate) slashes equity valuation multiples (especially high-growth tech), while (2) The skyrocketing short-term borrowing costs and banking contraction squeeze corporate earnings and consumer demand.
10. Explain Preferred Habitat Theory and how Central Bank Quantitative Easing (QE) exploits it. +

Answer: Preferred Habitat Theory states that market participants have specific natural maturity habitats dictated by their business models (e.g., life insurers need 30Y bonds; money market funds need 3M paper). They only deviate from their preferred sector if offered a compensatory price premium.

How QE Exploits Preferred Habitat:

  • During Quantitative Easing, the central bank buys hundreds of billions of dollars of long-dated bonds (7Y-30Y) directly from the market.
  • Because long-end investors (pension funds, insurers) are partially segmented, the central bank's massive concentrated buying removes duration supply from that specific habitat.
  • This directly drives up long bond prices and crushes long-term yields and term premia, forcing investors into corporate credit and equities to find yield, even if expected short-term rates remain unchanged.
