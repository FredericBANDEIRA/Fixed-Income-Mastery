Callable & Putable Bonds (Embedded Options)

Many corporate and municipal bonds contain embedded options that alter the bond's cash flows depending on prevailing interest rates:

Embedded Option Bond Valuation Decomposition
The Phenomenon of Negative Convexity: A standard bullet bond exhibits positive convexity—its price rises faster as yields drop than it falls when yields rise. However, for a callable bond, as interest rates decline toward or below the coupon rate, the issuer is increasingly likely to exercise the call option (refinance at cheaper rates). The call price ($P_{\text{call}}$) acts as an effective price ceiling. The price appreciation compresses, causing the price-yield curve to become concave (negative convexity).

Mortgage-Backed Securities (MBS) & Prepayment Risk

Agency Mortgage-Backed Securities (issued by Ginnie Mae, Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac) represent claims on the cash flows of large pools of residential mortgage loans. Unlike Treasuries, mortgage borrowers have the legal right to prepay all or part of their mortgage principal at any time without penalty.

Refinancing halts. Mortgage cash flows dry up, locking the investor into a low-yielding bond with extending duration.

Collateralized Mortgage Obligations (CMO) Tranching

Pass-through MBS pass all prepayment uncertainty directly to investors. To accommodate institutional investors with strict duration needs (e.g. short-term banks vs long-term pension funds), Wall Street structures Collateralized Mortgage Obligations (CMOs), carving the pool's cash flows into tailored tranches.

Asset-Backed Securities (ABS) & Loss Waterfalls

Asset-Backed Securities (ABS) pool non-mortgage consumer and corporate debts—such as auto loans, credit card balances, student loans, and equipment leases. The originator sells these receivables to a bankruptcy-remote Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV), which issues rated debt tranches.

Internal Credit Enhancement Mechanisms:
  1. Subordination: Tranches are tiered into Senior (AAA), Mezzanine (BBB/A), and Subordinated/Equity (First-Loss). Any collateral defaults are absorbed by the bottom tranches first.
  2. Overcollateralization (O/C): The total face value of collateral loans exceeds the debt issued (e.g. $105M of auto loans funding $100M of bonds).
  3. Excess Spread: The interest collected on consumer collateral (e.g. 9.5% on auto loans) exceeds the interest paid to ABS bondholders (e.g. 5.5%). The remaining spread covers credit losses and builds reserve funds.

Option-Adjusted Spread (OAS) & Spread Hierarchy

When analyzing bonds with embedded options or path-dependent cash flows (such as MBS and callable debt), traditional spread measures fail because future cash flows vary with the interest rate path.

The Spread Hierarchy & Option Cost
Spread Measure Definition Accounts for Curve Shape? Accounts for Optionality?
Nominal Spread $YTM_{\text{bond}} - YTM_{\text{Treasury}}$ No (single benchmark point) No
Z-Spread (Zero-Volatility) Constant spread added to each spot rate to match price Yes (entire spot curve) No (assumes fixed cash flows)
OAS (Option-Adjusted) Constant spread added to an interest rate tree or Monte Carlo paths to match market price Yes (entire term structure) Yes (strips out option value)
Interpreting Option Cost: $$\text{Option Cost (bps)} = \text{Z-Spread} - \text{OAS}$$ For a callable bond or MBS, the issuer/homeowner holds the option to prepay or call. Therefore, the investor requires a higher Z-spread to compensate for this risk ($\text{Z-Spread} > \text{OAS}$, so $\text{Option Cost} > 0$). If interest rate volatility rises, the call option expands in value, widening the gap between Z-spread and OAS!

Structured Fixed Income Interview Flashcards

10 essential interview questions on callable bonds, negative convexity, MBS prepayments, CMO waterfalls, ABS securitization, and Option-Adjusted Spread (OAS).

1. Explain negative convexity in the context of callable bonds and MBS. +
A conventional bullet bond has positive convexity—as interest rates drop, its price rises at an accelerating rate. However, when interest rates decline on a callable bond or MBS, borrowers refinance early to lower their borrowing costs.

This early redemption prevents the bond price from rising significantly above the call/par price. The price appreciation slows and flattens out, causing the price-yield curve to become concave ($\frac{d^2P}{dy^2} < 0$). In summary:
  • When yields drop, price gains are compressed.
  • When yields rise, price losses are fully experienced.
Desk Tip: Negative convexity is why MBS desks constantly dynamically hedge with Treasury futures or swaptions—they must sell duration when rates fall and buy duration when rates rise.
2. What are Contraction Risk and Extension Risk in Mortgage-Backed Securities? +
Prepayment optionality exposes the MBS investor to two asymmetric risks:
  • Contraction Risk (Rates Fall): Homeowners accelerate mortgage refinancings. Cash flows return to the investor much earlier than expected, forcing them to reinvest capital at prevailing lower yields (reinvestment risk).
  • Extension Risk (Rates Rise): Homeowners stop prepaying and hold onto low-rate mortgages. The expected life and duration of the MBS extends significantly just when interest rates are high and bond prices are crashing.
Desk Tip: The combination of contraction and extension risk is the direct operational manifestation of negative convexity.
3. How do SMM, CPR, and the PSA benchmark relate to each other? +
  • SMM (Single Monthly Mortality): The percentage of remaining pool principal prepaid in a single month.
  • CPR (Constant Prepayment Rate): The annualized equivalent of SMM: $$\text{CPR} = 1 - (1 - \text{SMM})^{12}$$
  • PSA Benchmark (Public Securities Association): A standardized industry curve assuming CPR ramps up linearly by 0.2% per month for the first 30 months until reaching 6.0% CPR, after which it stays constant at 6.0%.
A speed of $150\%$ PSA means CPR ramps to $1.5 \times 6\% = 9.0\%$ CPR at month 30.
Desk Tip: Remember: SMM is monthly; CPR is annual. PSA is an entire 30-year curve of CPRs scaled by a percentage factor.
4. Walk me through a basic Sequential-Pay CMO structure. +
A Sequential-Pay CMO carves an MBS collateral pool into ordered tranches (e.g. Tranche A, B, C):
All tranches receive coupon interest monthly based on their outstanding principal. However, all principal payments (both scheduled amortization and prepayments) flow exclusively to Tranche A until its principal is completely retired. Once Tranche A is paid off, principal flows to Tranche B until retired, and then to Tranche C.

This protects Tranche C from contraction risk (since Tranche A absorbs all early principal), while giving Tranche A short, bank-friendly duration.
Desk Tip: Sequential tranching redistributes prepayment risk among investors without eliminating it from the total collateral pool.
5. What is a PAC tranche and how does a Companion tranche protect it? +
A Planned Amortization Class (PAC) tranche provides cash flow certainty by adhering to a predetermined principal amortization schedule, provided that the actual collateral prepayment speed remains within a defined PAC Collar (e.g. 100% to 250% PSA).

How the Companion protects it:
  • If prepayments accelerate, the excess principal is absorbed by the Companion (Support) tranche.
  • If prepayments slow down, principal is diverted away from the Companion tranche to keep the PAC on schedule.
The PAC enjoys positive convexity inside the collar, while the Companion bears extreme negative convexity.
Desk Tip: If prepayments bust through the collar (e.g. 350% PSA), the companion is retired early ("busted companion"), and the PAC loses its cash flow protection.
6. What is Option-Adjusted Spread (OAS) and how does it differ from Z-spread? +
The Z-Spread is the constant spread added to the Treasury spot curve to discount a bond's contractual cash flows to match its market price, assuming cash flows never change.

The Option-Adjusted Spread (OAS) models the interest rate optionality using an interest rate tree or Monte Carlo simulation. It calculates cash flows along hundreds of interest rate paths, adjusting for prepayments and calls, and finds the constant spread required across all paths to match the market price.

$$\text{Option Cost} = \text{Z-Spread} - \text{OAS}$$ OAS strips out the embedded option, leaving the pure compensation for credit, liquidity, and term risk.
Desk Tip: When evaluating whether an MBS is cheap or rich, traders always compare OAS, never nominal spread or Z-spread.
7. If interest rate volatility surges, what happens to the OAS and price of a callable bond? +
If market volatility ($\sigma$) increases:
  1. Option Value Increases: The embedded call option held by the issuer becomes more valuable ($\text{Value of Call} \uparrow$).
  2. Bond Price Falls: Since $\text{Price}_{\text{callable}} = \text{Price}_{\text{bullet}} - \text{Price}_{\text{call}}$, the callable bond price falls.
  3. Spread Behavior: If the bond's market price has not dropped yet, the model's calculated OAS will collapse (or turn negative) because the increased option cost consumes more of the raw Z-spread: $\text{OAS} = \text{Z-Spread} - \text{Option Cost} \downarrow$.
Desk Tip: An investor long a callable bond or MBS is short volatility ($\text{Vega} < 0$).
8. What is the role of a Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV) in ABS securitization? +
A Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV) is a bankruptcy-remote legal entity created solely to purchase loans from the originator and issue asset-backed securities to investors.

Key Benefit: If the originating bank or auto finance company goes bankrupt, the collateral inside the SPV is shielded from the originator's creditors. This "true sale" allows the senior ABS tranches to obtain AAA credit ratings even if the originating company is rated BBB or in financial distress.
Desk Tip: The SPV isolates asset credit risk from corporate operating risk.
9. Explain the three primary internal credit enhancements in structured finance. +
  • 1. Subordination (Tranching): Junior/Equity tranches absorb initial credit losses before senior classes take any hit.
  • 2. Overcollateralization (O/C): The loan pool principal exceeds the debt securities issued (e.g. $105M loans backing $100M ABS debt), creating a 5% instant equity buffer.
  • 3. Excess Spread: The interest rate earned on underlying consumer loans (e.g. 10%) exceeds the weighted coupon paid to ABS bondholders plus servicing fees (e.g. 6%). The 4% excess spread covers monthly default losses and funds cash reserve accounts.
Desk Tip: Rating agencies model worst-case economic depression scenarios to verify that senior AAA tranches survive even if all excess spread and subordination are wiped out.
10. Why do Agency MBS trade at a spread over Treasuries even though they have zero credit risk? +
Ginnie Mae securities are backed by the full faith and credit of the US government, and Fannie/Freddie carry an explicit/implicit government guarantee. Despite essentially zero default risk, Agency MBS trade at a 100 to 180 bps spread over benchmark Treasuries due to:
  1. Prepayment / Convexity Risk: Investors demand yield compensation for bearing negative convexity, contraction risk, and extension risk.
  2. Hedging Costs: Institutional holders must continuously rebalance duration hedges.
  3. Liquidity & Balance Sheet Frictions: MBS require complex operational pooling, monthly factor accounting, and tie up bank leverage ratio capacity.
Desk Tip: When interest rate volatility spikes, the MBS spread over Treasuries widens dramatically to compensate for higher prepayment uncertainty.
