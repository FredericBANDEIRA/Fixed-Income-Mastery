Duration & Convexity

Macaulay center-of-gravity, modified price sensitivity, effective duration for options, and portfolio immunization

The Center of Gravity of Bond Cash Flows

Developed by Frederick Macaulay in 1938, Macaulay Duration ($D_{\text{mac}}$) is the weighted-average time until a bond's cash flows are received, where each cash flow is weighted by its present value as a proportion of the bond's total price.

Macaulay Duration Formula (in Years)

Key Structural Properties

  • Zero-Coupon Bonds: A zero-coupon bond has only one cash flow, paid at maturity $T$. Therefore, $D_{\text{mac}} = T$ exactly (e.g., a 10Y Treasury STRIP has a Macaulay duration of exactly 10.0 years).
  • Coupon Bonds: Because interim coupons arrive earlier than the final principal, $D_{\text{mac}} < T$ for all coupon-bearing bonds.
  • Coupon Effect: A higher coupon rate shifts more of the cash flow weight to earlier years, strictly reducing duration.
  • Yield Effect: A higher yield-to-maturity discounts distant cash flows more heavily, shifting relative weight to near-term coupons and reducing duration.
⚖️
Mechanical Analogy: The Fulcrum. Imagine the timeline of a bond as a seesaw. Each cash flow is a weight equal to its present value ($PV_t$). The Macaulay duration is the exact point on the timeline where you must place the fulcrum so that the seesaw balances in perfect equilibrium!

Effective Duration: Bonds with Embedded Options

Modified duration relies on a critical assumption: cash flow timing and amounts are invariant to interest rate shifts. This assumption completely breaks down for:

  • Callable Bonds: When yields plunge, the issuer calls the bond early, capping upside price appreciation.
  • Mortgage-Backed Securities (MBS): Homeowners refinance when rates drop (prepayment option), shortening the cash flow horizon.
  • Putable Bonds: Investors put the bond back to the issuer when yields soar, establishing a price floor.

Convexity: The Second-Order Curvature Adjustment

Because the price-yield relationship of an option-free bond is strictly convex (curved like a parabola rather than a straight line), the linear Modified Duration approximation always underestimates the price for any yield change!

Classical Redington Immunization

Defined by F. M. Redington in 1952, balance sheet immunization protects an institutional fund (e.g. pension fund or life insurer) from parallel shifts in the yield curve by matching assets to future liabilities:

Condition Mathematical Requirement Economic Objective
1. Present Value Match $PV(\text{Assets}) \ge PV(\text{Liabilities})$ Ensure the current portfolio is fully funded.
2. Duration Match $D_{\text{mod}}(\text{Assets}) = D_{\text{mod}}(\text{Liabilities})$ First-order price sensitivities cancel out under any parallel rate move ($\Delta PV_A = \Delta PV_L$).
3. Convexity Dominance $C(\text{Assets}) > C(\text{Liabilities})$ Asset value curve bends above the liability curve. For any rate move (up or down), assets outperform liabilities!

Bullet vs. Barbell Portfolio Construction

An investor with a 7-year target duration can build a Bullet (100% in 7Y bonds) or a Barbell (e.g., 50% in 2Y bonds and 50% in 20Y bonds). Because cash flows are more dispersed in a barbell, the Barbell has strictly higher Convexity for the identical duration!

Sales & Trading, Structuring & CFA L1/L2 Interview Flashcards

These questions are core staples in fixed income trader, asset management, and quantitative structuring interviews.

1. What is duration, and what are its two distinct interpretations? +

Answer: Duration has two distinct but mathematically related interpretations:

  1. Time Measure (Macaulay Duration): The weighted-average maturity of cash flows, measured in years. It represents the "economic life" or the cash flow center-of-gravity of the bond.
  2. Price Sensitivity Measure (Modified Duration): The percentage change in bond price for a 100 bps (1.0%) parallel shift in yield: $$\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{\text{mod}} \times \Delta y$$
Trader Tip: On trading desks, when someone says "duration", they almost always mean Modified Duration or DV01 (dollar duration risk).
2. Explain the mathematical relationship between Macaulay and Modified duration. +

Answer: Taking the first derivative of the standard bond pricing formula $P = \sum \frac{CF_t}{(1 + y/k)^t}$ with respect to the yield $y$:

$\frac{dP}{dy} = -\frac{1}{1 + y/k} \sum \frac{t/k \cdot CF_t}{(1 + y/k)^t} = -\frac{P \cdot D_{\text{mac}}}{1 + y/k}$

Dividing both sides by $-P$:

D_{\text{mod}} = -\frac{1}{P} \frac{dP}{dy} = \frac{D_{\text{mac}}}{1 + y/k}

Where $k$ is the payment compounding frequency per year. Under continuous compounding, $D_{\text{mod}} = D_{\text{mac}}$ exactly.

3. If a bond has a modified duration of 6.5 years and yields rise by 75 bps, what is the estimated price change? +

Answer: Using the first-order approximation:

\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{\text{mod}} \times \Delta y

$\Delta y = +75\text{ bps} = +0.0075$. Therefore:

\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -6.5 \times (+0.0075) = -0.04875 = \mathbf{-4.875\%}

The bond price will fall by approximately 4.88%. Because of positive convexity, the actual loss will be slightly smaller than this linear estimate.

4. Why do callable bonds and agency MBS exhibit negative convexity at low yields? +

Answer:

  • The Embedded Call Option: A callable bond is economically equivalent to a long position in a bullet bond plus a short call option granted to the issuer: $\text{Price}_{\text{callable}} = \text{Price}_{\text{bullet}} - \text{Price}_{\text{call option}}$.
  • Upside Capping: When interest rates drop significantly, the call option moves deep in-the-money. The issuer will exercise the call to refinance debt at lower rates. This creates a hard ceiling at the call price.
  • Prepayment Risk in MBS: For US Agency Mortgage-Backed Securities (MBS), homeowners refinance and prepay their mortgages when borrowing rates fall. The investor gets paid back early at par right when reinvestment yields are worst.
  • Curvature Flips: Because price appreciation is capped on rallies while downside risk remains unhedged on selloffs, the second derivative $\frac{d^2P}{dy^2}$ turns negative, resulting in negative convexity.
5. What is Effective Duration, and why must it be used instead of Modified Duration for MBS and structured notes? +

Answer: Modified duration mathematically assumes that the timing and magnitudes of future cash flows do not depend on the level of interest rates.

For mortgage-backed securities (MBS) and callable debt, cash flows are path-dependent and yield-contingent. Falling rates accelerate prepayments, shortening the cash flows; rising rates cause prepayments to halt ("extension risk"), lengthening the cash flows.

Effective Duration uses an option-pricing model (such as a Monte Carlo interest rate tree or prepayment model) to reprice the security under an upward and downward rate shock ($\Delta y$):

D_{\text{eff}} = \frac{P_- - P_+}{2 \times P_0 \times \Delta y}

This captures both yield changes AND cash flow alterations simultaneously.

6. What are the three mathematical conditions for classical Redington balance sheet immunization? +

Answer: To protect an institutional balance sheet (surplus $S = A - L$) against small parallel yield curve shifts:

  1. Solvency Condition: $PV(\text{Assets}) \ge PV(\text{Liabilities})$. The fund must be fully funded today.
  2. First-Order Neutrality: $D_{\text{mod}}(\text{Assets}) = D_{\text{mod}}(\text{Liabilities})$ (or in dollar terms: $\text{DV01}_A = \text{DV01}_L$). The initial price changes of assets and liabilities offset each other ($\frac{dS}{dy} = 0$).
  3. Convexity Dominance: $C(\text{Assets}) > C(\text{Liabilities})$. This guarantees that $\frac{d^2S}{dy^2} > 0$. The asset price curve has greater upward curvature than the liability curve, ensuring that the portfolio surplus increases for any non-zero parallel rate shock!
7. All else equal, does a higher coupon rate increase or decrease duration? What about maturity and YTM? +

Answer:

  • Higher Coupon → Decreases Duration: Larger interim coupon cash flows arrive earlier in the bond's life, pulling the cash flow "center of gravity" forward in time.
  • Higher Maturity → Increases Duration: For bonds trading at par or premium, extending maturity pushes the redemption principal further into the future, strictly lengthening duration.
  • Higher YTM → Decreases Duration: A higher discount rate penalizes distant cash flows exponentially more than near-term cash flows, shifting relative present value weight to the early coupons.
8. Explain the Barbell vs. Bullet portfolio strategy in terms of duration and convexity. +

Answer: Consider two portfolios structured to have the exact same duration of 7 years:

  • Bullet Portfolio: 100% invested in 7-year intermediate notes.
  • Barbell Portfolio: 50% invested in 2-year short bills and 50% invested in 20-year long bonds.

Because convexity is proportional to the square of time ($t^2$), the 20-year bond in the barbell generates massive convexity. Consequently, the Barbell will have significantly higher Convexity than the Bullet despite having the identical duration!

Tradeoff: The Barbell wins in volatile, large-shift rate environments, but typically yields less due to the "cost of convexity" (lower carry/roll-down).

9. What is Dollar Duration (DV01) and how is it used on trading desks to execute a duration-neutral hedge? +

Answer: DV01 (Dollar Value of an 01) is the dollar loss or gain experienced by a position for a 1 basis point ($0.01\%$) increase in yield:

\text{DV01} = D_{\text{mod}} \times \text{Market Value} \times 0.0001

Hedging Application: If a desk is long $100M of 10Y Treasuries with a DV01 of $78,000, and wants to hedge using 5Y Treasury futures where each contract has a DV01 of $42:

N = \frac{\text{DV01}_{\text{portfolio}}}{\text{DV01}_{\text{contract}}} = \frac{\$78,000}{\$42} \approx 1,857 \text{ contracts to short.}

10. Can Macaulay duration ever be negative? Can Effective duration ever be negative? +

Answer:

  • Macaulay Duration: Can NEVER be negative for standard long bond holdings because time $t > 0$ and all cash flows $CF_t \ge 0$. The weighted average of positive numbers is strictly positive.
  • Effective Duration: CAN be negative! A famous example is an Interest-Only (IO) mortgage strip. When mortgage rates rise, homeowners stop refinancing. The stream of interest payments lasts much longer than expected, significantly increasing total undiscounted cash flows. As a result, the price of an IO strip actually RISES when interest rates rise ($D_{\text{eff}} < 0$).
