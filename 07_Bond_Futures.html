CME Treasury Futures Contracts & Quotation

US Treasury futures traded on the Chicago Board of Trade (CME Group) are among the most liquid financial instruments on Earth. Unlike cash bonds which mature at fixed dates, a single futures contract represents a commitment to deliver or accept delivery of an eligible Treasury note or bond from a defined deliverable basket.

Standard Notional Coupon: All CME Treasury futures are standardized to a 6.00% annual coupon (semi-annual compounding). Delivery Cycle & Timeline

CME Treasury contracts trade on the quarterly March, June, September, December cycle (tickers H, M, U, Z). Key delivery dates:

  • First Position Day: Two business days prior to the first business day of the delivery month. Longs must declare position status.
  • First Notice Day: One business day prior to the first business day of the delivery month. Shorts can announce intention to deliver.
  • First Delivery Day: First business day of the delivery month. Physical delivery of cash bonds against cash payment can occur.
  • Last Trading Day: 7th business day prior to the last business day of the delivery month. Trading in expiring futures ceases.
  • Last Delivery Day: The last business day of the delivery month.

Conversion Factors (CF) & Invoice Pricing

Conversion Factor Definition

Intuition & Rules of Thumb:

  • If a bond's coupon $C = 6.00\%$, its yield to maturity at 6% is exactly par ($1.0000$), so $\text{CF} = 1.0000$.
  • If a bond's coupon $C > 6.00\%$, the bond trades at a premium when discounted at 6%, so $\mathbf{\text{CF} > 1.0000}$.
  • If a bond's coupon $C < 6.00\%$, the bond trades at a discount when discounted at 6%, so $\mathbf{\text{CF} < 1.0000}$.

Cheapest-to-Deliver (CTD) Analytics & Basis

Because the seller (short position) holds the right to choose which eligible bond to deliver, they will choose the bond that minimizes their delivery cost. This bond is known as the Cheapest-to-Deliver (CTD).

Gross Basis vs Net Basis (Basis Net of Carry - BNOC)
CTD Selection Rules:
  • Rule 1 (Net Basis): The CTD is the bond that minimizes Net Basis (BNOC). In an efficient market with no arbitrage, the CTD's net basis is typically near zero (or slightly positive due to delivery options).
  • Rule 2 (Implied Repo Rate - IRR): The CTD is the bond that maximizes the Implied Repo Rate. An arbitrageur financing a cash-and-carry trade earns the highest return by buying the CTD.

Basis Trading & Delivery Options

Basis Trading is one of the foundational trading activities on any Treasury trading desk or fixed income relative-value hedge fund. It exploits the spread between a cash bond and its equivalent futures position.

Long the Basis (Cash & Carry)
Buy Cash Bond + Sell Futures
Finance bond in repo market. Profitable if Implied Repo Rate (IRR) > Actual Repo Rate.
Short the Basis (Reverse Cash & Carry)
Short Cash Bond + Buy Futures
Lend cash via reverse repo. Profitable if actual repo rate > Implied Repo Rate (IRR).

The Four Embedded Delivery Options of the Short

Because the futures contract grants four asymmetric privileges to the short position, Treasury futures trade at a discount to theoretical forward value, creating positive Net Basis (the cost of these embedded options):

1. The Quality Option (Switch Option) +
The short can choose which bond from the basket to deliver. If market yields surge or collapse before delivery, the identity of the CTD switches. The short can purchase whichever bond has become cheapest and deliver it.
2. The Timing Option +
The short can choose on which business day during the delivery month to initiate delivery. If carry is positive (coupon yield > repo financing rate), the short will hold the bond and collect positive carry until the end of the month. If carry is negative, the short will deliver on the very first delivery day.
3. The Wild Card Option +
Treasury futures pit trading in Chicago halts at 2:00 PM CST, locking in the official settlement price. However, the short has until 8:00 PM CST to submit a Notice of Intent to Deliver. Meanwhile, the cash bond market continues trading in New York until 4:00 PM CST (5:00 PM EST). If cash bonds plummet in price after 2:00 PM (e.g. following late economic news or Treasury auction announcements), the short can buy cheap cash bonds in the open market and deliver them at the higher locked-in 2:00 PM futures settlement price!
4. The End-of-Month Option +
Trading in Treasury futures terminates 7 business days prior to month-end, freezing the final settlement price. However, physical delivery can take place up through the final business day of the month. The short effectively gets an unhedged 7-day look-back put option on the entire deliverable basket.

Treasury Portfolio Hedging with Futures

In Sales & Trading and asset management, portfolio managers use Treasury futures to rapidly adjust portfolio duration or fully immunize a bond portfolio against interest rate shocks. Because futures trade without upfront principal, they offer instantaneous execution with minimal balance sheet friction.

Futures Hedge Ratio Formula (DV01 / PV01 Matching)
The CTD Link: A futures contract's sensitivity is governed by the Cheapest-to-Deliver bond: $$\text{DV01}_{\text{futures}} \approx \frac{\text{DV01}_{\text{CTD}}}{\text{CF}_{\text{CTD}}}$$ Therefore, to hedge a cash portfolio of size $V$ and duration $D_{\text{port}}$, the required number of futures contracts $N$ is: $$N = - \frac{\text{DV01}_{\text{portfolio}}}{\text{DV01}_{\text{futures}}} = - \frac{\text{DV01}_{\text{portfolio}} \times \text{CF}_{\text{CTD}}}{\text{DV01}_{\text{CTD}}}$$

Fixed Income S&T Interview Flashcards

10 essential interview questions on Treasury futures, CTD analytics, conversion factors, basis trading, and desk hedging.

1. What is a CME Treasury futures Conversion Factor and why is it necessary? +
Because physical deliverable Treasuries in the marketplace have diverse coupons and maturities, CME standardizes the futures contract around a hypothetical 6.00% annual coupon. The Conversion Factor (CF) represents the clean price of $1.00 face value of the deliverable bond if it were priced to yield exactly 6.00% to maturity on the first day of the delivery month.

If a bond's coupon is greater than 6%, its CF is > 1.0; if its coupon is less than 6%, its CF is < 1.0. This scales the invoice amount so high-coupon bonds receive higher delivery proceeds, neutralizing coupon bias.
Desk Tip: Remember: CF is fixed for a contract month and does NOT change with market yields. It is published by CME prior to contract launch.
2. How do you calculate the cash invoice price received by the short upon delivery? +
The invoice price received by the short seller when delivering a bond is: $$\text{Invoice Price} = (\text{Futures Settlement Price} \times \text{Conversion Factor}) + \text{Accrued Interest}$$ For a standard $100,000 contract, multiply the decimal price per $100 par by 1,000.
Desk Tip: The buyer pays accrued interest up to the delivery date to the seller, exactly as in a secondary cash bond trade.
3. What is the Cheapest-to-Deliver (CTD) bond and how is it identified? +
The Cheapest-to-Deliver (CTD) is the bond in the deliverable basket that minimizes the net cost of delivery for the short position. Mathematically, it is identified as:
  1. The bond that minimizes Net Basis (Basis Net of Carry - BNOC).
  2. The bond that maximizes the Implied Repo Rate (IRR).
  3. Rough proxy: The bond with the lowest Gross Basis ($\text{Cash Price} - \text{Futures} \times \text{CF}$).
Desk Tip: In practice, traders look at the Implied Repo Rate. The CTD offers the highest IRR for a cash-and-carry trade.
4. How does the CTD switch when market yields rise well above 6% versus falling below 6%? +
This is the famous 6% Crossover Rule:
  • When Yields > 6.00%: The CTD tends to be the bond with the highest duration (longest maturity, lowest coupon). Because CF discounts at 6%, when yields rise above 6%, cash bond prices fall faster than CF-adjusted futures prices, making high-duration bonds cheaper.
  • When Yields < 6.00%: The CTD tends to be the bond with the lowest duration (shortest maturity, highest coupon).
Desk Tip: If the market trades near 6%, small yield twists cause frequent CTD switches, making futures tracking error and gamma spikes acute.
5. What is the difference between Gross Basis and Net Basis (BNOC)? +
$$\text{Gross Basis} = \text{Cash Clean Price} - (\text{Futures Price} \times \text{CF})$$ $$\text{Net Basis} = \text{Gross Basis} - \text{Carry}$$ Where $\text{Carry} = \text{Coupon Income Earned} - \text{Financing Repo Cost}$.

Gross basis simply reflects the raw cash-futures spread. Net basis deducts the cost or benefit of holding (carrying) the bond to the delivery date. In no-arbitrage equilibrium without delivery options, Net Basis should be zero.
Desk Tip: When traders say "the basis", they almost always mean Net Basis (BNOC). A positive net basis represents the premium paid for the short's delivery options.
6. What is the Implied Repo Rate (IRR) and how does an arbitrageur trade it? +
The Implied Repo Rate (IRR) is the annualized rate of return earned by buying a cash bond, simultaneously selling futures against it, and delivering the bond at maturity. It is the financing rate that sets Net Basis to exactly zero.

Arbitrage Rule:
  • If $\text{IRR} > \text{Actual Repo Rate}$: Cash-and-Carry Arbitrage (Buy Cash, Short Futures, finance at repo). The trader locks in an arbitrage spread equal to $\text{IRR} - \text{Repo}$.
  • If $\text{IRR} < \text{Actual Repo Rate}$: Reverse Cash-and-Carry (Short Cash, Buy Futures, lend cash in reverse repo).
Desk Tip: Treasury markets are so efficient that IRR rarely exceeds general collateral repo (GC) by more than 1–2 bps after transaction costs, except during repo squeezes.
7. What is the Wild Card delivery option? +
The Wild Card Option arises from a 6-hour timing mismatch on CME:
Futures trading halts at 2:00 PM CST, fixing that day's settlement price. However, the short position has until 8:00 PM CST to submit a delivery notice.

If cash Treasury prices tumble between 2:00 PM and 4:00 PM (e.g. after a bad 30Y Treasury auction or Fed announcement), the short can buy cash bonds at depressed prices in the late market and deliver them at the higher 2:00 PM locked settlement price, banking riskless profit!
Desk Tip: This option has positive value for the short. Hence, futures prices trade slightly lower than pure forward parity to reflect this short privilege.
8. How do you calculate the hedge ratio to immunize a $100M portfolio using 10Y futures (ZN)? +
The hedge ratio matches DV01 (dollar duration): $$N = - \frac{\text{DV01}_{\text{portfolio}}}{\text{DV01}_{\text{futures}}} = - \frac{\text{DV01}_{\text{portfolio}} \times \text{CF}_{\text{CTD}}}{\text{DV01}_{\text{CTD}}}$$ Example: If Portfolio DV01 is $\$68,000$, and the CTD bond has $\text{DV01} = \$68.50$ per $\$100,000$ par with $\text{CF} = 0.8845$: $$\text{DV01}_{\text{futures}} = \frac{\$68.50}{0.8845} = \$77.44$$ $$N = - \frac{\$68,000}{\$77.44} = -878 \text{ contracts (Short 878 ZN Lots)}$$
Desk Tip: If market yields change significantly, the CTD might switch to a bond with different duration and CF, necessitating dynamic re-hedging.
9. Why do Treasury futures typically trade at a discount to theoretical forward parity? +
Theoretical forward parity states: $$\text{Forward Price} = \frac{\text{Cash Spot Price} - \text{PV}(\text{Coupons})}{\text{DF}}$$ However, Treasury futures trade below this parity because the short holds four embedded options:
  1. Quality (Switch) Option: Option to switch CTD if yields move.
  2. Timing Option: Option to pick any delivery day in the month.
  3. Wild Card Option: 2:00 PM to 8:00 PM price drop exploitation.
  4. End-of-Month Option: Unhedged 7-day lookback put option after futures trading halts.
The combined value of these options reduces the futures price, which is why Net Basis is positive.
Desk Tip: When rates volatility ($\sigma$) surges, the value of these delivery options expands, causing Net Basis to widen.
10. Walk me through a Long the Basis trade: structure, risks, and monetization. +
Structure: Buy the CTD cash bond and simultaneously sell the corresponding Treasury futures contract in the conversion factor ratio ($1 / \text{CF}$). The cash bond is funded in the repo market.

Economics: The trader locks in the gross basis minus repo financing. As expiry approaches, gross basis inevitably converges toward zero. If the trader holds until delivery, they deliver the bond and collect the Implied Repo Rate.

Risks:
  • CTD Switch Risk: If yields move violently, the bond held might cease to be CTD. A new bond becomes CTD, and the held bond will trade at an unfavorable basis discount upon delivery.
  • Repo Squeeze: Financing rates in the repo market might spike above the Implied Repo Rate, turning carry deeply negative.
Desk Tip: Basis traders rarely hold to delivery; they trade basis volatility and unwind when net basis compresses toward the delivery option floor.
