DV01: The Currency of Rates Trading Desks

While Modified Duration expresses interest rate risk as a percentage, real trading desks and risk managers manage books in Dollar Terms. The standard market metric is DV01 (Dollar Value of an 01, or BPV — Basis Point Value), which measures the exact dollar change in position value for a 1 basis point ($0.01\% = 0.0001$) shift in yield:

Institutional Rule of Thumb: A 10-year par Treasury note has a modified duration of approximately $7.8\text{ years}$. For a $100,000,000 position, its DV01 is:
\text{DV01} = 7.8 \times \$100,000,000 \times 0.0001 = \mathbf{\$78,000\text{ per basis point}}
If 10Y yields jump by 15 bps following an employment print, the desk takes an immediate mark-to-market loss of $\$78,000 \times 15 = \mathbf{\$1,170,000}$.

Key Rate Durations (KRD): Non-Parallel Curve Risk

Standard duration assumes parallel yield curve shifts. In reality, yield curve movements are almost never parallel — steepening, flattening, and butterfly twists occur daily. A portfolio manager can be perfectly duration-hedged against a parallel shift and still suffer catastrophic losses from a curve steepener!

Hedging with Treasury Futures & The CTD Mechanism

Treasury futures (traded on the CME) are the most liquid derivatives in the world for hedging interest rate risk. Because CME Treasury futures allow the short counterparty to deliver any Treasury bond meeting maturity criteria from a basket of deliverable securities, pricing is tethered to the Cheapest-to-Deliver (CTD) bond.

The Conversion Factor ($CF$) & Hedge Ratio Formula

Sales & Trading, Structuring & CFA L1/L2 Interview Flashcards

These questions frequently appear in fixed income trader, strategist, and quantitative structuring interviews at top investment banks.

1. What is DV01 and how do you calculate it from modified duration? +

Answer: DV01 (Dollar Value of an 01) measures the absolute dollar price change of a bond or portfolio for a 1 basis point ($0.01\% = 0.0001$) shift in yield.

\text{DV01} = D_{\text{mod}} \times \text{Market Value} \times 0.0001 = -\frac{dP}{dy} \times 0.0001

For example, if a bond has a market value of $1,050 and a modified duration of 7.2 years:

\text{DV01} = 7.2 \times \$1,050 \times 0.0001 = \$0.756 \text{ per bond.}

2. How do you hedge a bond portfolio with Treasury futures, and what is the role of the Conversion Factor? +

Answer: To construct a DV01-neutral hedge:

N^* = -\frac{\text{DV01}_{\text{portfolio}}}{\text{DV01}_{\text{future}}} = -\frac{\text{DV01}_{\text{portfolio}}}{\text{DV01}_{\text{CTD}} / CF} = -\frac{\text{DV01}_{\text{portfolio}} \times CF}{\text{DV01}_{\text{CTD}}}

Role of the Conversion Factor ($CF$): The CME allows delivery of various eligible Treasury bonds against the futures contract. The Conversion Factor mathematically normalizes the price of each deliverable bond to trade as if it yielded exactly 6.00%. Multiplying by $CF$ adjusts the portfolio hedge for the specific CTD bond that drives the contract's price sensitivity.

3. What is Key Rate Duration (KRD) and why is parallel duration insufficient for risk management? +

Answer: Parallel duration assumes the entire yield curve moves uniformly up or down by the same number of basis points. In practice, non-parallel shifts (curve steepening, flattening, and butterfly twists) account for significant daily P&L volatility.

Key Rate Duration (KRD) measures sensitivity to an isolated 100 bps shift at a specific maturity node (e.g. 2Y, 5Y, 10Y, 30Y) while holding other key rates constant.

Trader Tip: A portfolio with zero net parallel duration can suffer catastrophic losses under a steepener if it holds a barbell (long 2Y, short 30Y). Only KRD or PCA factor risk monitoring reveals this hidden exposure.
4. A portfolio has a DV01 of $50,000. Benchmark yields rise by 25 bps. What is the approximate P&L? +

Answer: Since DV01 represents the dollar price drop per +1 basis point yield increase:

\text{Dollar P\&L} \approx -\text{DV01} \times \Delta y_{\text{bps}}

\text{Dollar P\&L} \approx -\$50,000 \times (+25) = \mathbf{-\$1,250,000}

The portfolio will experience an immediate loss of approximately $1.25 million.

5. Explain Basis Risk in a Treasury futures hedge. What are its primary sources? +

Answer: Basis risk is the risk that the price of the hedging instrument (Treasury futures) does not move in perfect correlation with the asset being hedged (cash bond portfolio).

Primary Sources:

  1. Credit & Spread Basis: Corporate or municipal bond spreads over Treasuries may widen while Treasury yields fall, resulting in losses on both the cash bond and the short hedge.
  2. CTD Switch Risk: If yields move substantially, a different bond in the deliverable basket can become Cheapest-to-Deliver (CTD), abruptly altering the duration and conversion factor of the future.
  3. Curve & Tenor Mismatch: Hedging a 7-year bond with a 10-year Treasury future leaves residual exposure to the $7\text{Y}-10\text{Y}$ curve slope.
  4. Futures Roll Basis: The calendar spread pricing when rolling expiring futures contracts forward to the next delivery quarter.
6. What is the Cheapest-to-Deliver (CTD) bond in a Treasury futures contract, and what determines it? +

Answer: The seller of a Treasury futures contract has the option to deliver any eligible Treasury bond from the deliverable basket. The Cheapest-to-Deliver (CTD) is the specific bond that minimizes the net cost of delivery (or maximizes the Implied Repo Rate):

\text{Basis} = \text{Clean Cash Price} - (\text{Futures Price} \times CF)

Key Rules of Thumb:

  • When market yields are above 6.00%, the CTD is typically the longest-maturity, lowest-coupon deliverable bond.
  • When market yields are below 6.00%, the CTD is typically the shortest-maturity, highest-coupon deliverable bond.
7. Why do the sum of all Key Rate Durations equal the total Modified Duration of a bond? +

Answer: Consider a total yield curve shift function $\Delta y(t)$. By definition, total duration is the directional derivative of bond price with respect to a uniform shift across all maturities: $\Delta y(t) = \Delta y$ for all $t$.

In KRD modeling, any continuous curve shift $\Delta y(t)$ is represented as a linear combination of triangular key-rate basis functions: $\Delta y(t) = \sum_{k=1}^K w_k(t) \cdot \Delta y_k$.

When all key rate shifts are set to the identical value $\Delta y_k = \Delta y$, the triangular basis functions sum to exactly 1 at all points along the timeline ($\sum w_k(t) = 1$). Therefore, the price change from a parallel shift is identical to the sum of the individual key rate price changes:

\frac{\Delta P}{P} = \sum_{k=1}^K \left(-\text{KRD}_k \times \Delta y\right) = -\left(\sum_{k=1}^K \text{KRD}_k\right) \Delta y \implies \sum_{k=1}^K \text{KRD}_k = D_{\text{mod}}

8. How does a rates desk execute a duration-neutral steepener trade using 2Y and 10Y Treasury futures? +

Answer: In a steepener trade, the trader profits if the $10\text{Y}-2\text{Y}$ spread widens (short rates fall relative to long rates):

  1. Legs: Buy 2Y (ZT) futures (long front-end duration) and Sell 10Y (ZN) futures (short long-end duration).
  2. DV01-Neutral Weighting: To eliminate exposure to overall market direction (parallel shifts), the trade must have zero net DV01: $$\text{DV01}_{\text{ZT}} \times N_{\text{ZT}} = \text{DV01}_{\text{ZN}} \times N_{\text{ZN}}$$
  3. Ratio Calculation: If ZT has a DV01 of $38.50 and ZN has a DV01 of $71.50: $$\frac{N_{\text{ZT}}}{N_{\text{ZN}}} = \frac{\$71.50}{\$38.50} \approx 1.86$$ The desk buys approximately 186 ZT contracts for every 100 ZN contracts sold.
9. What is PV01 in interest rate swaps, and how does it relate to the swap's DV01? +

Answer: In interest rate swaps, PV01 is the present value of a 1 basis point ($0.01\%$) coupon annuity on the swap's notional over its remaining life:

\text{PV01} = \text{Notional} \times 0.0001 \times \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i DF_i

Because the floating leg of a newly struck swap resets to par ($100$) on every payment date, its interest rate sensitivity between payment dates is near zero. Therefore, the DV01 of an interest rate swap is almost identical to its PV01:

\Delta \text{MtM} \approx \text{PV01} \times \Delta S

Where $\Delta S$ is the change in the benchmark par swap rate in basis points.

10. What is Taylor Series scenario stress testing, and why must second-order convexity be included for large macro shocks? +

Answer: Taylor series expansion models the price change of a bond portfolio as a polynomial function of yield shocks:

\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{\text{mod}} \times \Delta y + \frac{1}{2} C \times (\Delta y)^2

For small moves ($\pm 5\text{ bps}$), the linear duration term dominates ($>98\%$ of the move). However, in macroeconomic crisis stress testing (e.g. $+150\text{ bps}$ central bank shock or $-200\text{ bps}$ pandemic easing):

  • The quadratic term $(\Delta y)^2$ grows rapidly ($(0.02)^2 = 0.0004$).
  • Ignoring convexity for long-duration portfolios creates massive errors ($>300\text{ bps}$ in price), drastically overstating losses on rate hikes and understating gains on rallies.
