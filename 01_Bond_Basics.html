Bond Basics

Pricing, accrued interest, day-count conventions, clean vs dirty price

📄 What Is a Bond?

A bond is a debt instrument where the issuer borrows funds from investors and agrees to pay periodic interest (coupons) and return the face value (principal) at maturity. Bonds are the primary tool for governments and corporations to raise debt capital.

Key Components

  • Face Value (Par) — The nominal amount repaid at maturity, typically $1,000 or $100
  • Coupon Rate — Annual interest rate applied to face value (e.g., 5% on $1,000 = $50/year)
  • Coupon Frequency — Semi-annual (US Treasuries, corporates), annual (EUR govvies), quarterly (some floaters)
  • Maturity Date — When the principal is repaid. Short (≤3Y), medium (3–10Y), long (10Y+)
  • Issuer — Government (sovereign), corporate, municipal, supranational
💡 A bond is nothing more than a package of known future cash flows. Pricing a bond = discounting those cash flows back to today.

📅 Why Day-Count Conventions Matter

Day-count conventions determine how interest accrues between two dates. They define the fraction of a year used to compute coupon payments, accrued interest, and discount factors. Different markets use different conventions — knowing which one applies is critical for pricing and settlement.

⚠️ Using the wrong day-count convention is one of the most common errors in fixed income. Always verify the market convention before pricing.

📐 The Four Main Conventions

ACT/ACT (ISDA or ICMA)

Uses actual days in both numerator and denominator. Most accurate, used for US Treasuries and most sovereign bonds.

ACT/ACT Day Fraction

30/360 (US Bond Basis)

Assumes every month has 30 days and every year has 360 days. Simplifies calculations. Used for US corporate bonds and agency bonds.

30/360 Day Fraction

ACT/360

Actual days in numerator, 360 in denominator. Used in money markets (T-bills, CP, repo, SOFR, EURIBOR).

ACT/360 Day Fraction

ACT/365 Fixed

Actual days in numerator, always 365 in denominator (even in leap years). Used for GBP markets (gilts, SONIA).

ACT/365 Day Fraction

Bonds are quoted at their clean price (without accrued interest), but settled at their dirty price (clean price + accrued interest). This avoids price jumps on coupon dates.

📐 The Price-Yield Relationship

A bond's price is the present value of all its future cash flows, discounted at the yield to maturity (YTM). Price and yield move in opposite directions — this is the most fundamental relationship in fixed income.

Bond Price (Semi-Annual Coupon)

Premium, Par, and Discount

  • Premium: Coupon > YTM → Price > Par (investors pay more for the higher income stream)
  • Par: Coupon = YTM → Price = Par (exactly fairly priced)
  • Discount: Coupon < YTM → Price < Par (lower coupon compensated by capital gain)
💡 Pull to par: As maturity approaches, a bond's price converges to par regardless of whether it trades at a premium or discount. This is because fewer coupon cash flows remain to justify any premium/discount.

🎯 Key Interview Questions

Click any question to reveal the answer. These cover the most common fixed income interview questions on bond basics.

What is the relationship between bond price and yield? +

Bond price and yield have an inverse, convex relationship. When yields rise, bond prices fall — and vice versa. This is because the bond's fixed cash flows are discounted at a higher rate, reducing their present value. The relationship is not linear but convex: a 1% drop in yields increases the price by more than a 1% rise in yields decreases it.

Explain the difference between clean price and dirty price. +

Clean price is the quoted price excluding accrued interest — it reflects pure market value. Dirty price (full price) = Clean price + Accrued interest — it is the actual amount the buyer pays. Bonds are quoted clean to eliminate the saw-tooth pattern caused by accruing interest, but settled dirty because the buyer must compensate the seller for interest earned since the last coupon.

How is a zero-coupon bond priced? +

A zero-coupon bond pays no coupons — it is issued at a discount and pays face value at maturity. Its price is simply the present value of the face value: P = F / (1 + y)^T. The investor's return comes entirely from the difference between the purchase price and par. Zeros have the highest duration for their maturity because there are no interim coupon cash flows to shorten the weighted average time of payment.

Why does a premium bond converge to par at maturity? +

A premium bond trades above par because its coupon is higher than the market yield — investors pay extra for the above-market income. As maturity approaches, fewer coupon periods remain to justify the premium. At maturity, the bondholder receives exactly par, so the price must converge to 100. This is called "pull to par" — the premium amortizes over the life of the bond. The same logic applies in reverse for discount bonds (price converges up to par).

What is the difference between ACT/360 and 30/360? +

ACT/360 uses actual calendar days in the numerator and 360 in the denominator. It is used in money markets (T-bills, repo, SOFR). Because the actual year has 365 or 366 days, ACT/360 slightly overstates interest compared to actual. 30/360 assumes every month has 30 days and every year has 360 days. It is used for US corporate bonds. The key difference: 30/360 smooths out month-length differences while ACT/360 captures actual elapsed time. Over the same period, they can give different day fractions.

All else equal, does a higher coupon increase or decrease a bond's price sensitivity to yield changes? +

A higher coupon decreases price sensitivity (duration). With a higher coupon, more of the bond's value comes from near-term cash flows (the larger coupons), shifting the weighted average time of cash flows forward. This makes the bond shorter in duration terms. Zero-coupon bonds, which have no interim cash flows, have the highest duration for a given maturity.

What is a floating-rate note and how does its price behave? +

A floating-rate note (FRN) pays coupons that reset periodically to a reference rate (e.g., SOFR + spread). Because the coupon adjusts to market rates, the FRN's price resets to approximately par on each coupon date. Between reset dates it can deviate slightly, but its duration is effectively just the time to the next reset (often 3 months). FRNs have near-zero interest rate risk, making them attractive when rates are rising.

If two bonds have the same YTM but different maturities, which one is more sensitive to rate changes? +

The longer-maturity bond is more sensitive to rate changes. It has higher duration because its cash flows are spread over a longer period — the distant cash flows are discounted more heavily, and small changes in the discount rate compound over more periods. This is why long-end bonds (20Y, 30Y) move much more in price for a given yield change than short-end bonds (2Y, 5Y).

Explain what "trading in 32nds" means for US Treasuries. +

US Treasuries are quoted in points and 32nds of a point. For example, 99-16 means 99 and 16/32 = 99.50% of par. A "tick" is 1/32 = 0.03125% of par. For a $1,000 face value bond, one tick = $0.3125. Some quotes add a "+" which means half a tick (1/64). For example, 99-16+ = 99 + 16.5/32 = 99.515625%. This convention dates back to when fractions were easier to compute mentally than decimals.

What risks does a bondholder face beyond interest rate risk? +

Beyond interest rate risk (the primary risk for government bonds), bondholders face: Credit risk — the issuer may default or be downgraded; Liquidity risk — the bond may be hard to sell at fair value; Reinvestment risk — coupons may be reinvested at lower rates; Inflation risk — real returns erode if inflation exceeds expectations; Call risk — the issuer may redeem callable bonds early when rates fall; Currency risk — for foreign-denominated bonds. For corporates, credit risk is usually the dominant factor beyond rates.
