Yield Measures & Term Structure

Spot rates, forward curves, bootstrapping algorithms, and pricing relative to call dates

The Spectrum of Fixed Income Yields

A single bond generates several distinct yield metrics depending on cash flow assumptions, embedded options, and compounding conventions. In Sales & Trading and Structuring, confusing these metrics leads to misquoted trades, unhedged reinvestment exposure, and severe option-adjusted pricing errors.

1. Current Yield (CY)

Measures annual cash income as a percentage of the current market price. While simple and quick to calculate mentally, it ignores capital gains/losses at maturity, reinvestment, and the time value of money.

Current Yield Formula

2. Yield to Maturity (YTM)

The Internal Rate of Return (IRR) of all promised cash flows assuming the bond is held to maturity and default does not occur.

Yield to Maturity (IRR) Definition
⚠️
The Critical YTM Assumption: Reinvestment Risk. YTM is only earned in reality if every coupon is reinvested at the exact same YTM until maturity. If interest rates fall over the life of the bond, the realized return will strictly underperform the quoted YTM!

3. Callable Bonds: Yield to Call (YTC) & Yield to Worst (YTW)

When a bond is callable, the issuer has the embedded right (a short call option for the bondholder) to retire the bond before maturity at a specified call price.

  • Yield to Call (YTC): The internal rate of return calculated assuming the bond is called on the next call date at the call price $CP$.
  • Yield to Worst (YTW): The minimum yield among all possible call dates and maturity. If a callable bond trades at a premium, the call will likely be exercised, so $YTW = YTC < YTM$. If trading at a discount, the issuer is unlikely to call, so $YTW = YTM$.
Yield to Call Formula

4. Compounding Conventions: BEY vs EAY

Different markets quote yields using different compounding intervals. Comparing bonds across markets requires converting to a common standard:

  • Bond Equivalent Yield (BEY): Standard semi-annual compounding annualized by multiplying by 2 (nominal annual yield): $BEY = 2 \times y_{semi}$.
  • Effective Annual Yield (EAY): Full annual compounding reflecting the compounding of interim coupons.

The Spot Rate Curve: The Pure Discount Benchmark

A spot rate (or zero-coupon rate, denoted $s_t$ or $z_t$) is the annual rate of return earned on a single cash flow received at date $t$, with no intermediate coupon payments. The collection of spot rates across all maturities forms the zero-coupon yield curve.

Why Spot Rates Matter: The Coupon Bias Flaw in YTM

Yield to Maturity discounts every single cash flow of a bond (whether received in month 6 or year 30) at the exact same internal rate. But in reality, short-term money has a different cost than long-term money!

  • Two bonds with the same maturity but different coupon rates will have different YTMs even if both are completely default-free (e.g. US Treasuries). This distortion is called the coupon bias.
  • Discounting each cash flow using its respective maturity spot rate eliminates coupon bias and obeys the Law of One Price:

Treasury STRIPS: Practical Application of Spot Rates

In 1985, the US Treasury introduced the STRIPS program (Separate Trading of Registered Interest and Principal of Securities). Investment banks can physically "strip" a 10-year Treasury note into 20 coupon STRIPS and 1 principal STRIP, trading each as a zero-coupon bond. If the sum of the stripped zero-coupon bond values differed from the whole bond price, dealers would immediately execute an arbitrage transaction:

Market Condition Trading Desk Action Arbitrage Trade
Whole Bond Price > Sum of STRIPS Buy STRIPS, reconstitute into whole bond Sell whole bond at market, pocket difference
Whole Bond Price < Sum of STRIPS Buy whole Treasury bond, strip it into components Sell zero-coupon STRIPS in market, pocket difference

Spot Curve vs. Par Yield Curve Relationship

The par yield curve plots the coupon rate for bonds of each maturity priced exactly at par ($100). The mathematical relationship between the spot curve and par curve is strict:

  • Normal (Upward Sloping): Because a coupon bond receives coupons at lower short-term rates and the principal at the high long-term rate, its par yield is an average of short and long rates. Thus, the Spot Curve lies above the Par Curve.
  • Inverted (Downward Sloping): Short rates exceed long rates. The Spot Curve lies below the Par Curve.
  • Flat: Spot Curve and Par Curve are identical.

Implied Forward Rates & No-Arbitrage

A forward rate is an interest rate agreed upon today for a loan or investment that begins on a specified future date and matures at an even later date.

Forward rates are not magic forecasts; they are mathematical break-even rates implied by the current spot curve. Under the no-arbitrage condition, an investor with a 2-year horizon must earn the exact same total return whether they:

  1. Invest for 2 years today at the 2-year spot rate $s_2$, OR
  2. Invest for 1 year today at the 1-year spot rate $s_1$, and simultaneously lock in a 1-year forward loan from Year 1 to Year 2 at forward rate $f_{1,2}$ (often denoted $1\text{y}1\text{y}$).

Forward Curve vs Spot Curve Dynamics

  • When the spot curve is upward sloping, the 1-period forward curve lies above the spot curve ($f > s$).
  • When the spot curve is downward sloping (inverted), the forward curve lies below the spot curve ($f < s$).
  • When the spot curve is at its local peak/trough, the forward rate equals the spot rate ($f = s$).

Forward Rate Agreements (FRAs) & Market Trading

In institutional markets, forward rates are traded directly via Forward Rate Agreements (FRAs) or interest rate swap forward starting legs. In an FRA (e.g., a "$3 \times 9$" FRA spanning from month 3 to month 9):

  • The buyer pays the agreed fixed forward rate $R_K$ and receives the floating benchmark (e.g. SOFR/Euribor $L$).
  • Unlike futures, an FRA cash-settles at the beginning of the forward period ($T_1$), which means the payoff must be discounted back to the settlement date!

The Bootstrapping Algorithm

In financial markets, zero-coupon bonds (STRIPS or bills) only exist liquidly for short maturities (up to 1 year). For longer horizons (2Y, 5Y, 10Y, 30Y), Treasuries and benchmark interest rate swaps trade as coupon-bearing instruments at or near par.

Bootstrapping is the recursive mathematical algorithm used by trading desks and quantitative models to extract the theoretical zero-coupon spot yield curve from a set of observable coupon instruments.

Sales & Trading & Structuring Interview Flashcards

These questions are frequently asked in fixed income sales & trading, DCM (Debt Capital Markets), and structuring interviews at top tier investment banks, as well as on CFA Level 1 and Level 2 exams.

1. Walk me through the bootstrapping algorithm to construct a spot curve from par yields. +

Answer: Bootstrapping is an iterative, recursive procedure to derive zero-coupon discount factors from coupon-bearing par instruments:

  1. Anchor at the short end: The shortest maturity (e.g. 6-month bill or FRN) has no prior coupons. Its par yield is already its spot rate, giving the first discount factor: $DF_1 = \frac{1}{1 + y_1/2}$.
  2. Iterate through subsequent maturities: For maturity $n$, write the par bond price equation ($100 = \text{PV of cash flows}$). Every prior cash flow is discounted using already-solved discount factors $DF_1, \dots, DF_{n-1}$.
  3. Isolate the unknown: Solve algebraically for the final discount factor: $DF_n = \frac{100 - c_n \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} DF_j}{100 + c_n}$.
  4. Compute Spot Rates: Convert $DF_n$ into an annualized zero-coupon yield via $z_n = 2 \left( DF_n^{-1/n} - 1 \right)$. Repeat until the curve is built.
Trader Tip: Mention that in modern rates desks, bootstrapping is done on OIS (SOFR / ESTR) par swap curves rather than Treasury coupons to avoid collateral and credit funding basis distortions.
2. Why does the forward curve lie above an upward-sloping spot curve? +

Answer: Mathematically, the $T$-period spot rate is a geometric average of the successive 1-period forward rates:

$(1 + s_T)^T = (1 + s_1)(1 + f_{1,2})(1 + f_{2,3})\dots(1 + f_{T-1,T})$

Think of the spot curve as an average grade and the forward rate as the marginal grade on the next exam. For your cumulative GPA (the spot rate) to go up, your score on the next exam (the forward rate) must be strictly higher than your current GPA. Therefore, when the spot curve slopes upward, the forward curve must lie strictly above the spot curve.

3. What is the fundamental flaw of YTM, and why is it misleading for comparing two bonds? +

Answer: YTM has two major flaws:

  • Unrealistic Reinvestment Assumption: YTM assumes that all intermediate coupon cash flows can be reinvested at the exact same YTM until the bond matures. In a declining rate environment, reinvestment returns fall short, reducing the realized return.
  • Coupon Bias / Cross-sectional Distortion: YTM discounts cash flows across time at a single flat rate. Two bonds with the identical 10-year maturity but different coupon rates (e.g. 2% vs 8%) will have different YTMs even if priced off the identical spot curve. A portfolio manager comparing bonds purely by YTM might think the higher YTM bond is "cheaper," when in reality it simply has higher cash flows occurring at different points along the yield curve.
Structuring Tip: This is why desks use Z-spread (Zero-volatility spread) or asset swap spread over the zero-coupon curve, rather than simple yield spread over benchmark YTM, to price relative value.
4. If the 1Y spot rate is 4.00% and the 2Y spot rate is 5.00%, what is the 1-year forward rate 1 year from now (1y1y)? +

Answer: Using the discrete no-arbitrage formula:

$(1 + s_2)^2 = (1 + s_1)(1 + f_{1,2})$

Plugging in values:

$(1 + 0.05)^2 = (1 + 0.04)(1 + f_{1,2}) \implies 1.1025 = 1.04 \times (1 + f_{1,2})$

$1 + f_{1,2} = \frac{1.1025}{1.04} \approx 1.060096 \implies f_{1,2} \approx 6.01\%$

Quick mental math heuristic: $2 \times s_2 - 1 \times s_1 = 2(5.0\%) - 4.0\% = 6.00\%$. The linear approximation is remarkably close!

5. Explain the difference between BEY (Bond Equivalent Yield) and EAY (Effective Annual Yield). +

Answer:

  • BEY (Bond Equivalent Yield): A nominal annual yield based on semi-annual compounding. It takes the semi-annual periodic yield $y_{semi}$ and doubles it: $BEY = 2 \times y_{semi}$. It ignores the effect of compounding the first coupon into the second half of the year. US Treasuries and corporates are conventionally quoted on a BEY basis.
  • EAY (Effective Annual Yield): An annualized rate that fully accounts for compound interest over the full year: $EAY = (1 + y_{semi})^2 - 1 = (1 + BEY/2)^2 - 1$.

Because of compounding, for any positive yield, $EAY > BEY$. For example, a 6.00% BEY corresponds to $EAY = (1 + 0.03)^2 - 1 = 6.09\%$.

6. What is Yield to Worst (YTW) and how should an institutional investor interpret a callable bond trading at a premium? +

Answer: Yield to Worst (YTW) is the lowest possible yield that an investor can realize across all possible call dates and the final maturity date without the issuer defaulting.

When a callable bond trades at a premium ($Price > Par$), market interest rates have declined below the bond's coupon. Under these conditions:

  • The issuer has a powerful economic incentive to exercise their call option to retire high-coupon debt and refinance at prevailing lower rates.
  • If called early, the investor's premium is amortized over a much shorter period, dragging down their return.
  • Thus, for premium bonds, $YTC < YTM$, and $YTW = YTC$. Quoting the YTM to a client on a premium callable bond is misleading and can lead to execution disputes.
7. Why do US Treasury STRIPS trade at different yields than the parent whole Treasury bond? +

Answer: Even though both are backed by the full faith and credit of the US government:

  • Tax Asymmetry (Original Issue Discount - OID): In the US, phantom income tax must be paid annually on zero-coupon STRIPS as their value accretes toward par, even though no physical cash is received until maturity. This creates negative tax drag for taxable retail accounts.
  • Different Institutional Client Demands: Pension funds and life insurance companies heavily demand long-term Principal STRIPS to achieve maximum duration matching for long-dated liabilities, causing long STRIPS to trade at rich valuations (lower yields).
  • Liquidity Differentials: Whole benchmark Treasuries (especially on-the-run) have vastly higher secondary market liquidity and repo specialness than off-the-run individual coupon STRIPS.
8. How does a Forward Rate Agreement (FRA) settle, and why is the settlement discounted? +

Answer: In an FRA, two counterparties agree on a fixed forward rate $R_K$ for a forward period starting at $T_1$ and ending at $T_2$ (tenor $\tau = T_2 - T_1$).

  • On the fixing date $T_1$, the floating benchmark rate $L$ is observed.
  • If this were a standard interest payment, the interest differential $(L - R_K) \times \text{Notional} \times \tau$ would be paid at the end of the borrowing period ($T_2$).
  • However, by market convention, FRAs settle immediately at the start date $T_1$.
  • Because payment occurs $\tau$ periods early, the interest difference must be discounted back to $T_1$ using the prevailing floating rate: $$\text{Payoff at } T_1 = \text{Notional} \times \frac{(L - R_K) \times \tau}{1 + L \times \tau}$$
9. If the spot yield curve is inverted, where do forward rates lie relative to spot rates and par yields? +

Answer: In an inverted (downward sloping) yield curve environment:

Forward Rate < Spot Rate < Par Yield

Because the spot rate decreases with maturity ($s_2 < s_1$), the "marginal" forward rate needed to pull down the average must be even lower than the spot rate itself. Furthermore, a par bond receives intermediate coupons discounted at higher short-term rates, pulling the par yield above the zero-coupon spot rate for that maturity.

10. What is Realized Horizon Return, and how does it differ from YTM? +

Answer: Realized Horizon Return (also called Total Horizon Return) is the annualized compound return actually earned by an investor over an explicit holding period $H$, which can be shorter or longer than the bond's maturity:

  • It explicitly models: (1) coupon cash flows received, (2) the actual reinvestment rate at which those coupons were compounded, and (3) the sale price of the bond at date $H$ based on the future yield curve at that time.
  • Unlike YTM, which assumes a static holding to maturity with coupon reinvestment locked at the YTM, Horizon Return quantifies both reinvestment risk and capital price risk (exit yield).
